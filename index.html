Wall text: position

The grid is the plan. The cats are what actually happens. This page is a gallery built in the space between them.

What is ordered

My life runs on a grid. Two kids, four cats, Ben, a startup, a design master's degree: none of it works without a timetable. School pickup is a column. Feeding time is a row. I bake by feel weight, and I have a professional pastry diploma that says so: petits fours in rows of the same height, a Saint-Honoré made in six hours of steps in exactly the right order. So this page is built like the most ordered place I know, a museum. There is one way through, with rooms numbered from 0 to 8, a door before each one, and a floor plan at the side so you always know where you are. Every work has a wall label in the same order: name, title, year, material. Names are set in one serif, everything else in Helvetica, on a twelve-column grid. The clock in room 1½ shows the real time and the shape of my day in red dots.

What breaks it

Then someone takes a ballpoint pen to the walls. Every work here gets a doodle, and the doodles are bad on purpose: a crooked crown on the accepted passport photo, a stick cat drawn over Tova, a cross through the corn pizza that the label refuses to withdraw. The five rejected passport photos, the one at 7am, the one asleep, the one with the egg, hang beside the accepted one, because they are the parts of my life no grid has ever held. The cats can be dragged off their places, and they only come back halfway. The Acquire button sells you a work and never delivers it. And you get a pen too: the whole show can be drawn on, and room 8 is a blank wall for your own untitled piece.

Against templatology

Ask an AI for a personal designer website and you get the template: a centred hero, a purple gradient, three rounded cards, Inter, and a sentence about crafting delightful experiences. It looks organised, but nobody made a decision. The copy fits every designer, so it describes none. Müller-Brockmann's grid was a belief about clarity and honesty. The template is that belief with the belief taken out. So I borrowed the most serious template there is, the museum label, and filled it with things no model would guess: Untitled (Sauce), c. 1999. Cat on grid, not for sale. America, 2019, city unknown. The format is borrowed. Every word inside it is a decision.

The encounter

I don't think either side wins. A day where the plan survives untouched is perfect boring. A day with no plan is a disaster. My identity is the moment in between, and on this page it has a shape: a serious label and a bad doodle on the same wall. The label is what makes the doodle readable, and the doodle is what proves a person lives here. Even the side quest ends that way. You walk through my house in black and white pixels, bump into the cats and the oven, and the card is stamped Accepted. On the first try. Unlike some.

Your ticket. It filled itself in while you walked.
